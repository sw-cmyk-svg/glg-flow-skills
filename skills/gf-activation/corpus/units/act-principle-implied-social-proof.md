**Principle — implied social proof beats direct claims; leave a visible trace.**

Showing that a behavior is *common* is more persuasive than asserting your own popularity,
because it sidesteps the "well, you would say that" discount. Evidence distilled from the book:
- **HMRC tax letters (2012):** a plain legal-obligation message got **68%** compliance; adding
  "nine out of ten people in [your area] pay on time" raised it to **83%**.
- **Keizer & Lindenberg (2008):** a litter-free alley (implied norm "people tidy up") produced
  **33%** littering; a messy alley produced **69%** — a *more-than-doubling* from an implied
  norm alone, a bigger swing than the direct HMRC claim.
- **Peterson (2019):** labelling a missing product **"Sold Out"** (popularity) vs "Unavailable"
  cut shopper disappointment with the site by ~**15%**.

**How to apply.** Make real usage *visible* rather than boasting: activity feeds ("Acme's team
just shipped…"), "most teams start with this template," waitlist/"sold out" framing for capacity,
and shareable traces (public links, "made with" badges) — the digital equivalent of Aperol's
distinctive glass or the "I voted" sticker.

**When it applies.** Signup decision and first-run empty states, where uncertainty is highest.

**Caution (ethics).** Everything here must be **true** — real norms, real "sold out," real
activity. Manufactured scarcity, fake counts, or invented activity are dark patterns (document
only as anti-patterns). For bank/wallet and other financial archetypes, take care not to imply a
norm that normalizes risky financial behaviour, and advise the client to confirm compliance with
financial-promotion rules. Pairs with [[act-principle-social-proof]].
