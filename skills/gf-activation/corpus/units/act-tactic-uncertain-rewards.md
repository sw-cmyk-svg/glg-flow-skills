**Tactic — a *variable* reward motivates more than a fixed one (handle with care).**

An uncertain reward of equal expected value pulls harder than a guaranteed one. Evidence
distilled from the book:
- **Zeiler (1972):** pigeons pecked **~2×** as often when the pellet came unpredictably (50–90%
  of the time) than when guaranteed.
- **Mazar et al. (2017):** a vending promo — 50¢ fixed vs 75¢ with a 1-in-3 chance of free
  (equal expected value) — sold **84 vs 120** units, **+43%** for the uncertain option.

**How to apply (lightly).** For activation, a small *surprise-delight* on completing a real
setup step (an unexpected credit, a "you unlocked…" moment) can lift completion more than a
flat, advertised reward. Keep it a genuine delight, not a loop.

**When it applies / honest limit.** Multi-step setup where a nudge to finish helps. **Too little
reward backfires** — Zeiler's birds disengaged at 10–30% reward frequency, so a stingy,
mostly-nothing mechanic *reduces* motivation. If the activation path is already smooth, prefer
[[act-tactic-endowed-progress]] or [[act-principle-make-it-easy-friction]] first.

**Caution (ethics).** This is **gambling-style** psychology — the strongest guardrail case in
the corpus. Keep any use mild, genuine, and non-compulsive; never build a variable-reward loop
designed to create a habit against the user's interest. **For bank/wallet and other financial
archetypes, treat it with heightened care:** gamble-style mechanics around deposits, spending, or
wagering can fall under gambling and financial-promotion regulation — advise the client to confirm
regulatory and responsible-gambling compliance before using them, rather than reaching for them
off-hand. Regret lotteries and false scarcity are documented only as anti-patterns, never
prescribed. See `copilot/GUARDRAILS.md`.
