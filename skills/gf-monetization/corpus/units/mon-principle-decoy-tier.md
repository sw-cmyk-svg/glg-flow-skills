**Principle — a third option changes which of the other two people pick (the decoy effect).**

Adding a carefully placed option shifts choice between the existing ones. The classic demonstration is
*The Economist*'s subscriptions: offering a print-only option at the **same price** as print+web made
far more people choose the more expensive bundle, because it now looked like an obvious deal. The
underlying bias (asymmetric dominance; Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982) means a tier can be designed less to
sell than to make a **neighbouring tier look better** — the middle-tier or top-tier "nudge."

**How to apply (carefully).** You can use the structure legitimately: a higher "best" tier makes the
"better" tier read as sensible value (price relativity). Present tiers so the one that genuinely fits
most customers looks like the clear choice.

**When it applies.** Pricing-page design, tier ordering, add-on framing.

**Caution (ethics) — this is the line.** A decoy that exists **only** to manipulate — a tier no one
should ever rationally buy, placed purely to push another — is where legitimate framing becomes a dark
pattern. Keep every tier a **real, buyable option** some segment actually wants; if a tier has no
honest buyer, cut it. For regulated archetypes, misleading option design can breach consumer-protection
rules. Pairs with [[mon-principle-good-better-best]] and [[mon-principle-price-relativity-anchoring]].
