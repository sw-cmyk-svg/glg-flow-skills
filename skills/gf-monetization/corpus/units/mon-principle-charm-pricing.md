**Principle — a price ending in 9 reads as meaningfully cheaper than the round number above it (charm pricing).**

A 99-ending price feels disproportionately lower than one cent below the round figure. Evidence
distilled from the book: Avner Shlain (Berkeley, 2023) analysed retail scanner data across thousands
of products and found people perceived a 99-ending price as **15–25 cents lower** than the rounded
price, despite a one-cent real difference — so $5.99 "feels like" ~$5.75. The authors' own survey of
650 UK consumers found charm-priced items **9% more likely** to be judged good value. Amazon has used
a charm price on every Prime tier ($10.99, $14.99/mo).

**How to apply.** Where a price is currently round, test a 99-ending (or the local convention) on the
pricing page — it's a near-zero-cost change. It matters most on **self-serve, price-sensitive** tiers
where the buyer scans quickly; it matters less on enterprise/negotiated deals where a round number can
signal confidence.

**When it applies.** Self-serve PLG pricing pages, lower tiers, consumer-ish SaaS.

**Caution (ethics + honesty).** This is a **small, well-worn** effect — don't overstate it or build a
pricing strategy on it; it's a polish, not a lever. Never pair it with a fake "was" price or
manufactured discount (deceptive-pricing dark pattern, and unlawful in many jurisdictions). Pairs with
[[mon-principle-price-relativity-anchoring]].
