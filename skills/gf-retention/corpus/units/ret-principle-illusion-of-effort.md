**Principle — make the work the product does *for the user* visible, so they keep valuing it (illusion of effort).**

People use effort as a proxy for quality, and they value the same thing more when they can see the
work behind it. Evidence distilled from the book: Kruger et al. (2004) — the **same** poem was rated
**+10%** (5.84 → 6.43) and judged worth **+90%** ($50 → $95) when readers were told it took 18 hours
instead of 4. The authors' own study — a vodka bottle was liked by **17% → 23%** (a **+35%** lift)
when people were told 143 designs were tested first. Dyson built a brand on making its 5,127
prototypes visible. The lever for retention: a product that silently does a lot of work for the user
is under-valued unless that work is **surfaced**.

**How to apply.** Show the user the work the product is doing on their behalf — "scanned 2,400
listings," "analysed 18 months of your data," "checked 50 sources." Make background effort legible at
value moments (reports, dashboards, renewal). This reinforces the value they're getting and gives
them a concrete reason to stay, especially when the output itself is hard to judge.

**When it applies.** Products whose value is produced behind the scenes and easy to take for granted —
automation, monitoring, analysis, aggregation.

**Caution (honesty).** The effort must be **real** — surface work that genuinely happens, never
fabricate or inflate it (the book pairs this bias with the "danger of claimed data" for that reason).
Don't manufacture busywork or fake progress bars to seem harder-working; that's a dark pattern,
documented not prescribed. Pairs with [[ret-principle-sunk-cost]] and
[[ret-principle-stored-value-switching-costs]].
