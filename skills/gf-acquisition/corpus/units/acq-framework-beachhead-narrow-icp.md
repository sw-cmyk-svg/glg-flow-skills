**Framework — win one narrow segment completely before widening (the beachhead).**

The instinct is to target the largest possible market; Moore's prescription is the opposite —
**minimize the target until you've won it.** Pick one tightly defined segment (the *beachhead*),
dominate it fully, and use that win as a reference base and word-of-mouth engine to knock over
adjacent segments one at a time (the "bowling pin" sequence). Adjacent segments must be similar enough
that the first win reads as credible proof — and each new pin needs its own complete offer and its own
reference customers.

**How to apply.** Define the ideal customer profile narrowly enough that you can realistically be the
obvious best choice *for them* — a specific role, industry, or use case, not "SMBs" or "developers" in
general. Concentrate acquisition on that beachhead until you own it (strong word-of-mouth, reference
customers, a repeatable message), then expand to the nearest adjacent segment rather than everyone at
once. A narrow ICP sharpens the message and the channel choice too, so it compounds with positioning.

**When it applies.** Early-stage acquisition, a product spread thin across too many audiences, or a
message that's gone generic trying to speak to everyone. Pairs with
[[acq-framework-positioning-dunford]] (the target-customer component) and
[[acq-principle-message-market-fit]] (a narrow ICP makes the message land).

**Caution (honesty).** A beachhead can be chosen too small (no real volume) or too broad (no
dominance) — it's a judgement validated by whether you actually win the segment, not a formula.
Expansion isn't free: each adjacent segment is its own crossing. This is PLG-SaaS/tech framing; for a
regulated or very different archetype the segmentation logic holds but the segments and proof don't
transfer — flag borrowed priors.
