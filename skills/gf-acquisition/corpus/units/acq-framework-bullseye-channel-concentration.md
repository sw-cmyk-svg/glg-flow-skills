**Framework — test many channels cheaply, then concentrate on the one that works.**

At any given stage, **one traction channel tends to dominate a startup's customer acquisition** — not
a balanced portfolio. The Bullseye Framework (from *Traction*, 19 channels total) is the disciplined
way to find it without spreading thin. Five steps: **brainstorm** every channel, **rank** them into
Column A (promising now), B (possible), C (long-shot), **prioritize** down to about three to test,
**test** each with a cheap small-scale experiment (checking cost, available volume, and whether the
customers fit), then **focus** all resources on the winner until it saturates. Repeat when it does.
Thiel's framing, which the essay cites: "It is very likely that one channel is optimal." And the
blunt stakes: **poor distribution — not product — is the number one cause of failure.** (Mint focused
on blogs to reach ~40,000 users, then switched to PR when blogs maxed out, hitting ~1M within six
months.)

**How to apply.** Don't commit to a channel on a hunch and don't run ten at once. Rank all candidates,
pick ~3 to test with small real-money experiments, and judge them on cost per signup, how much volume
the channel actually holds, and whether those signups are the right users — then pour effort into the
single winner. The goal of the test round is to *find* the channel; the goal after is to *exploit* it.

**When it applies.** Early-stage channel discovery, a stalled growth number, or a founder running too
many half-committed channels. Use with [[acq-framework-channel-model-fit]] to seed Column A with only
channels your model can afford.

**Caution (honesty).** "One channel dominates" is a strong prior, not a guarantee — some products run
two. A cheap test can under-read a channel that needs scale or creative iteration to work, so size the
test to give the channel a fair chance before killing it. The channel you win on will decay (see
[[acq-principle-channel-saturation]]), so keep a next candidate warm.
