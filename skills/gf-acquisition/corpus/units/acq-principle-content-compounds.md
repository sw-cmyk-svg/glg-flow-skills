**Principle — a small share of content compounds: it keeps attracting signups long after publishing, so content is an appreciating asset, not a campaign.**

Most content decays (a spike at publish, then a slide), but a minority **compounds** — organic traffic
that keeps *rising* over time as it accrues search authority and links. HubSpot's analysis of its own
and customers' blogs found compounding posts were only about **10% of posts but produced roughly 38% of
total traffic** — a few evergreen pieces doing most of the work. The implication for acquisition: a
compounding piece pays back for years with no repeat spend (unlike a paid ad), so content strategy is
about finding and doubling down on the few durable, search-intent-driven topics — not publishing
volume for its own sake.

**How to apply.** Judge content by its *trajectory*, not its launch spike: find the pieces whose
organic traffic grows month over month, and invest in more like them (update them, build topic
clusters around them) rather than chasing a constant stream of new posts. Expect a long lag before a
piece compounds, and expect most pieces not to.

**When it applies.** Planning or assessing an SEO/content acquisition channel; setting expectations for
when content investment pays back.

**Caution (honesty).** The 10%/38% figures are **HubSpot's own study, ~2016, not independently
replicated** — use them as a *concept* (a minority of content drives most organic traffic), not a
promise or a current benchmark. Compounding is variable and slow; month-to-month traffic won't rise
smoothly, and most content never compounds. Pairs with [[acq-principle-owned-vs-rented-channels]] and
[[acq-tactic-seo-search-intent]].
