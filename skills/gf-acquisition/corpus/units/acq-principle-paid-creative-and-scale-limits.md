**Principle — on modern paid platforms, creative is the main lever and marginal CAC rises as you scale.**

Two realities govern paid acquisition today. First, on auction-and-algorithm platforms (Meta most
clearly), **the ad creative now drives more of the performance variance than manual targeting** — the
platform handles much of the targeting, so the creative is where the practitioner's leverage sits, and
creative **fatigues predictably** (rising frequency and falling clickthrough as an audience sees it
repeatedly). Second, **spend has diminishing returns**: as you pour more budget into a channel, the
*marginal* cost per acquisition rises (you exhaust the best-fit audience and bid into less-qualified
reach), so the average CAC hides a worse marginal CAC at the edge. Together: win on creative volume and
iteration, and budget to *marginal* efficiency, not the flattering blended average.

**How to apply.** Treat creative as a pipeline — produce and test new concepts continuously, expect
winners to fatigue, and retire them before they decay. When scaling a paid channel, watch the marginal
CAC of the *added* spend (what the last increment cost), not just blended CAC, and stop scaling where
the marginal cost exceeds what the business can pay back. Pair with CAC-payback discipline to set that
ceiling.

**When it applies.** Running or advising on any paid-social/paid-search channel, or explaining why a
paid channel's efficiency fell as budget grew.

**Caution (honesty).** This is a **qualitative** principle — the specific "2× spend → X% ROAS decline"
or "$30 → $40 CPA" numbers circulating in vendor/practitioner posts are anecdotal and **not** used here;
measure the client's own marginal curve. CAC has no canonical spec id — flag definitions. Creative
persuasion still carries the truthful-marketing line (no deceptive or fake-scarcity creative). Pairs
with [[acq-principle-channel-saturation]] (the same decay, at the channel level) and
[[acq-benchmark-cac-payback]].
