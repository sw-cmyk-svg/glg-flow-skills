## Claim
Every experiment must arrive with a measurable intent. Directed tests name
the specific business KPI they're expected to move; exploratory/discovery
tests (painted-door, demand gauges) don't need a revenue KPI but must name
the demand signal or learning question they're measuring. "Let's see what
happens," with no measurable intent of any kind, is a rejection — not a
prioritization question.

## Applicability — works when
- Screening any experiment proposal at intake, before it competes for
  traffic, time, or analyst attention
- The proposal is exploratory/discovery in nature (fake-door tests, demand
  gauges) — these are valid, but only when they name a specific learning
  goal or demand signal they'll measure
- A cosmetic or preference-driven change is proposed with no stated
  expected effect on any metric

## Backfires when
- The rule gets weaponized to kill legitimate discovery or fake-door tests
  that DO carry a real learning goal, just not a revenue KPI — rejecting
  these misapplies the standard
- Demanding a hard revenue KPI from a test whose entire purpose is learning
  (does demand exist for X?) — that's not the bar exploratory tests need to
  clear

## Growth applications
- A pricing-page test must name its expected effect on paying_conversion
  before it's scheduled, not after
- A nav or IA change needs a stated hypothesis about signup_rate or
  activation_rate, or it doesn't earn a slot
- A fake-door test for a new feature needs a named demand signal
  (click-through, waitlist signups) it's measuring — that's sufficient
  without a revenue KPI attached

## Practice stance
We screen every experiment proposal for a measurable hypothesis before it
enters the backlog — a named KPI for directed tests, a named demand signal
or learning goal for exploratory ones. We refuse "let's see what happens"
regardless of how cheap the change looks, and we refuse to demand a revenue
KPI from a test that was never meant to have one.
