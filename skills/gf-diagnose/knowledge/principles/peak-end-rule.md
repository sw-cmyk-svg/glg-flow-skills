## Claim
People remember an experience by its most intense moment (the peak) and
how it ended, largely disregarding its duration and averaging out
everything else. In the 1993 cold-pressor experiment, subjects preferred
to repeat a longer ice-water immersion that ended with a slightly
gentler final stretch over a shorter one that ended at its worst —
strictly more total discomfort, remembered as better. The 1996
colonoscopy field study confirmed the same pattern in real patients:
retrospective ratings tracked the peak and the ending, not the
procedure's duration. Well-replicated across affective-experience
research; graded B because most direct demonstrations are from
physical-discomfort paradigms, with product/UX extrapolation resting on
analogy rather than direct replication in that context.

## Applicability — works when
- The experience has a clear temporal arc with an identifiable ending
  (a project, a trial period, a support interaction, an onboarding flow)
- Memory of the experience (not moment-to-moment satisfaction) is what
  drives the next decision — renewal, referral, review
- You can control the peak or the ending without misrepresenting the
  overall experience

## Backfires when
- The middle of a flow is neglected in favor of polishing the ending —
  optimizing a flawless offboarding screen while the core weekly
  experience is mediocre doesn't fix retention, because the peak (or a
  string of bad moments) still dominates memory if it's the more intense part
- The ending is broken while the middle is fine — a confusing final
  invoice, an abrupt offboarding, or a trial that expires silently with
  no closing moment erases credit for everything that came before,
  regardless of how good the average experience was
- Manufacturing an artificial "peak" that doesn't reflect real value
  delivered reads as gimmicky once noticed

## Growth applications
- Design a deliberate high-value moment early in onboarding (a fast
  first win) as the peak, not just a smooth average (activation_rate,
  time_to_activation)
- Fix trial-expiry endings — a clear, well-timed close beats a silent
  lapse — before investing further in mid-trial nudges (trial_to_paid)
- Close out downgrade/cancellation flows with a genuinely helpful,
  non-manipulative ending, since it's the last impression before churn
  becomes a review or a referral decision (w4_retention)

## Practice stance
Before we spend effort smoothing the middle of a flow, we check whether
the ending is actually the broken part — it usually has outsized
leverage on remembered experience relative to its cost to fix. We
refuse to fabricate a peak moment that doesn't correspond to real
delivered value; the rule works because memory tracks genuine intensity,
not staged intensity.
