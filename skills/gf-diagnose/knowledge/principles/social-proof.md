## Claim
People infer correct behavior from what others like them are doing,
and shift their own behavior toward it — most strongly under
uncertainty, when the reference group is similar, and when the norm is
stated specifically rather than generically. The hotel towel-reuse field
experiments are the cleanest field evidence: "75% of guests in this room
reused their towel" outperformed generic environmental appeals by a wide
margin. Graded B rather than A because effect size depends heavily on
similarity of the reference group and specificity of the framing —
generic or dissimilar-group proof is weak.

## Applicability — works when
- The comparison group is similar to the viewer (peers, same industry,
  same company size) — proof from a dissimilar group barely moves anyone
- The user faces genuine uncertainty about what "normal" looks like
- The number or behavior stated is specific and true ("2,400 teams,"
  not "many teams")

## Backfires when
- Framed negatively — "many users struggle to set this up" or showing a
  low adoption number — normalizes the undesired behavior instead of
  discouraging it; descriptive norms cut in whatever direction the
  number points, including the wrong one
- The reference group reads as dissimilar or aspirational-only (huge
  logos on a page selling to a five-person startup can depress trust)
- The numbers are vague, rounded suspiciously, or unverifiable —
  skepticism kills the effect and taints the surrounding copy

## Growth applications
- Show adoption counts specific to the visitor's segment on the
  pricing or signup page (signup_rate)
- Surface "teams like yours activated X in their first week" during
  onboarding, only where true for that segment (activation_rate)
- Cohort-specific retention proof ("87% of teams your size are still
  active at week 4") on renewal or expansion touchpoints (w4_retention)

## Practice stance
We only ship social proof that's true for the specific segment on that
page — generic company-wide stats on a segment-specific page is the
single most common way this backfires. We never state a proof point in
the negative ("only 20% of users..."), even accurately, because the
descriptive-norm mechanism doesn't care about our intent.
