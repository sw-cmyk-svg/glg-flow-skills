## Claim
Perceived scarcity of an option increases desire for it — the classic
finding is real in offline field-sales contexts (limited stock, closing
offers). But the digital-marketing version of this claim (countdown
timers, "only 2 left" banners) rests on much thinner evidence than its
ubiquity suggests: the original studies weren't run on the interfaces
we now deploy it in, direct replications in e-commerce/SaaS contexts are
sparse and mixed, and the effect is known to be reactance-prone —
graded C because the everyday application is closer to folklore than to
a verified digital finding.

## Applicability — works when
- The scarcity or deadline is real and verifiable (actual limited
  capacity, an actual cohort close date, an actual price change)
- The audience is making a one-time or infrequent purchase decision,
  not a recurring one where they'll see the tactic repeatedly
- The stakes are low enough that reactance (the urge to resist being
  pushed) doesn't dominate

## Backfires when
- Fake countdowns, resetting timers, or false stock counts are
  discovered — trust damage is durable and spreads to the rest of the
  page's claims, not just the urgency element
- Sophisticated or B2B buyers, who are primed to detect manipulation,
  penalize the vendor rather than respond to the pressure
- Repeat exposure across sessions kills the effect entirely and can flip
  it negative — a permanent "sale ends soon" banner reads as a lie by
  week two

## Growth applications
- Genuine cohort-based enrollment deadlines for an onboarding cohort or
  live program (signup_rate) — only if the deadline is real
- True capacity constraints on a limited beta or founder-tier plan
  (new_signups)
- Real, non-repeating promotional windows tied to an actual pricing
  change (paying_conversion)

## Practice stance
Given the C grade, we treat scarcity/urgency as a last-resort lever, not
a default, and we will not fabricate it under any circumstance — every
deadline or stock count we show must be independently verifiable by us
before it ships. When we do use it, we test it once per audience segment,
not as a permanent fixture, because the evidence for durable effect at
digital scale simply isn't there.
